The Well of Saint Aelfwynn

So beautiful - towers of gold, as the dawn rays of the rising sun envelop the abbey on the hill.

The town is waking to the chill of early spring, murmurs of the townsfolk drifting on the cold, damp morning air. I turn back to the causeway west, the timbers slick with mould and dew; I must be mindful of my steps so as not to slip. The effort centres me, and I pull my woollen shawl tighter. Ahead, I see the long treeline of the Weald beyond the marshes.

I am now at the forest's edge. I clear the little stone shrine of grass and twigs, the pool gleaming below it in the sunlight. All around, offerings decorate the well: rosaries, poppets from the children, ribbons tied to tree branches. Each a thanksgiving, a wish, or perhaps just a hope for a beloved. I recognise one rosary carefully placed on the stone arch - a distinctive colour and pattern. Is it Edith's? My heart flutters and I blush, through concern for a dear friend. I should respect her privacy, but I hope the lady can help her.

I fill a small green glass bottle with water from the well, for Sister Hilda. Then I kneel in the long grass and pray for her blessing on the townsfolk - for the sick, the old, and the young.

Aelfwynn died here, long before the abbey was built. A woman not of the Weald, from a place far beyond the shire, she took this scrap of land between the woods and marsh - a wild edge-land - as her home. She built the first church, bringing hope to a ravaged country.

She was found one morning, as if sleeping, in the long grass. Some say the children of the forest took her back, that she was their lost sister. Where she was found, a spring soon welled up - her gift to her flock, to heal them, to keep them safe.

My mind wanders. I must return to the abbey and the warmth of the kitchen, my bones aching from the years, and my stomach from the night's fast.
