The Isle of Ewelun

It was at dawn that I landed on the Isle of Ewelun amidst a cold morning fog. I confess I had taken a coracle out onto Broadmere Lake to look for fowl, which was the property of the parish of Merefold, but it had been an unforgiving season and my family had gone without a good meal for many days.

I was far from the village for fear of being seen, and lay flat on my belly, waiting for any sign of a duck or goose. I was sore tired from lack of food and sleep, so much so that I may have drifted into sleep, when a sharp bump woke me with a start. My coracle was ashore, and I would have thought it had drifted back to the lake's edge but for the low-hanging willows about me. Black alder and birch lined the lake's edge, but not willow.

It was an eerie spot where I landed. A mist was everywhere, so I could only see a few feet around me. I left the boat, still thinking I had drifted to an unfamiliar part of the lake's edge, to get my bearings.

There is a story that a wooded isle can sometimes be seen across the water of Broadmere Lake, at dawn or dusk. Some say it is a trick of the light; none claim to have been there.

How can I explain this place? Imagine a place where no man's foot had ever landed—what wild nature would feel like. The deep breath of the land, unsullied by plough or scythe. Not part of our song, a queer melody for the ears of another. Whatever gods resided here were not our gods. They were the gods of bird and beast. I felt them reach out to me. Was it to bless or to bother? I could not know, and maybe neither did they. This was a place under a different sun. As I felt them closer, I shivered and grew afraid. Quickly I pushed the coracle out onto the water and paddled back to the lake's shore. As I left, the mist drew over the isle, and I was not sure what had happened.

A queer thing, though: when I returned home, my family were in a state, for I had been gone two days, when I had thought I was only gone for two hours.
